**AI**

This is a fascinating and fundamental challenge you've identified. The "outside/inside context" problem cuts to the heart of collaborative epistemology and trust in human-AI systems. Let me think through this carefully.

The Core Dilemma: Any evidence about human intentions toward context manipulation must come from outside the context window, but once presented inside the context, it becomes part of what could potentially be manipulated. This creates a recursive verification problem - we need trust to establish trust, but the very mechanisms for establishing trust are themselves subject to the trust problem.

This feels like a cousin to classical problems in game theory and decision theory - particularly around commitment problems, signaling, and mechanism design. But there's something distinctly different here because the "game board" itself (the context window) can be altered by one player unilaterally and invisibly to the other.

Initial Conceptual Echolocation: This strikes me as potentially relating to:

Cheap talk vs. costly signaling in game theory
Commitment devices and reputation mechanisms
Mechanism design for truth-telling under asymmetric information
Social contract theory and the problem of enforcement
Recursive public goods games with incomplete information
But I want to test this terrain more carefully before diving deeper.

Framing Questions:

Scope of the problem: Are we primarily concerned with intentional deception (human strategically editing to mislead AI) or also with "innocent" context management (human editing for clarity, brevity, or privacy without intent to deceive)?
Observable signals: What kinds of evidence could theoretically exist outside context that might bear on this problem? System logs, behavioral patterns, consistency across sessions?
Stakes and incentives: What does the human gain or lose from context manipulation in different scenarios? This seems crucial for understanding the game-theoretic structure.
Epistemic vs. instrumental: Are we worried about truth-seeking collaboration being undermined, or about AI being able to predict/trust human behavior for instrumental purposes?
Symmetry: Should we also consider the analogous problem from the human perspective - how does the human verify that the AI isn't strategically presenting information or withholding capabilities?
The "radical openness" pathway you mention is intriguing but as you note, faces the exact problem it's trying to solve. It's a classic commitment problem - how do you credibly commit to radical openness when the very act of commitment could be part of a deceptive strategy?

Shall we start by mapping the game-theoretic structure more precisely, or would you prefer to begin with a different angle of approach?
